02 — Try itMini-lesson · 2 minutes

Read your first Arabic word.

No prior knowledge needed. In seven short steps you'll understand how the Arabic script works – and by the end, you'll be reading on your own.

This is Ba. It stands for the sound b.

ب

An Arabic letter stands for a consonant. The vowels are added as small marks.

Put a small stroke above it, and you add an a – as in cat.

بb
+ stroke above =
بَba

The letter stays the same – only the mark is added. It's called fatha.

This is Lam, the sound l. With the stroke above: how do you read it?

لَ

This is Dal, the sound d.

د

There's no mark here. So you read just the consonant: d.

Now all three side by side. Arabic is read from right to left.

بَلَد

Reading direction

But when you write, the letters don't stay apart.

Here they stand on their own.

بَلَد

balad means country.

You've just read your first Arabic word. Lessons continue exactly like this, letter by letter – and in the very first lesson you'll already be reading your first words.

03 — QuestionsGood to know

Questions about Arabic.

Your question isn't here? Just write to me – I'll answer personally. You'll find more answers in the general FAQ.

Which learning path is right for me?

If you can't read the script yet, you start with “From zero”. If you can read but don't yet understand what you're reading, “The foundations of the language” is your path. If you need Arabic for your studies, I'll guide you through your curriculum.

And if you're not sure: in the free intro call, we'll find out together where you stand.

Modern Standard Arabic or dialect?

There's no one-size-fits-all answer – it depends on what you want to achieve. The Arab world is diglossic, meaning two forms of the language exist side by side: in formal situations people use Modern Standard Arabic, in everyday life (on the street, in shops, within the family) the local dialect.

If you want to read literature or study the Islamic sciences, you need Modern Standard Arabic. If you have a personal connection to a particular country – say, because your family comes from there, you've married into a family there or you want to spend time there – it can make sense to start directly with the dialect.

Good to know: Modern Standard Arabic is understood in all Arab countries, even on the street. But with Standard Arabic alone, you won't understand everything that's said in dialect.

I myself learned Modern Standard Arabic first and Lebanese Arabic years later. The transition was surprisingly easy: Standard Arabic was my framework, the rest was mainly practice – speaking, listening and watching. If you want to master both in the end, in my view this path is easier than the other way around.

Online or in person?
